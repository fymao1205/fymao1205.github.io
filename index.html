Welcome!

Thanks for visiting. I'm a biostatistician who develops methods for designing cost-efficient studies when exposures are expensive to measure, and for analyzing incomplete follow-up data such as censored or intermittently observed outcomes. Much of my work so far has been in cancer screening and prevention, and I'm excited to bring these tools to new areas of health research. Here you'll find my research, publications, talks, and software. I'm always happy to hear from potential collaborators and students, so please feel free to reach out.

About

I am a Tenure-Track Assistant Professor in the Department of Biostatistics at the University at Buffalo (SUNY). My research focuses on two-phase sampling designs, lifetime data analysis, multistate modeling, and statistical methods for cancer screening and disease natural history studies. I develop principled statistical methodology and open-source software to support cost-effective study designs in cancer epidemiology and translational research.

Research Interests

Large cohorts and disease registries hold rich information on how chronic diseases develop, but the measurements that matter most, such as novel biomarkers, are expensive, and disease status is often observed only at irregular clinic visits. My research develops statistical methods that make the most of such data: designs that decide whom to measure, and models that recover how disease progresses from incomplete observation. I apply these methods to cancer prevention, with the goal of informing screening and management decisions.

Cost-Efficient Study Designs

When an assay can be run on only a fraction of stored specimens, two-phase designs choose that fraction to maximize statistical efficiency. I develop such designs for settings where standard methods break down:

  • Outcomes known only as current status or through intermittent visits (Stat Med 2023)
  • Populations in which some individuals will never experience the event (Biometrics 2024)
  • Pooling multiple registries with different recruitment and truncation schemes (JRSS-C 2024)
  • Evaluating new cancer screening tests on archived samples (Biometrics 2026)
Disease Natural History & Multistate Models

Understanding how precancers arise, persist, regress, and progress is central to deciding when to screen and whom to treat. I build multistate and latent-variable models for these processes:

  • Data-augmented multistate models that combine cross-sectional and longitudinal studies of HPV and cervical precancer (Biostatistics 2026)
  • Microsimulation of HPV natural history for comparing novel cervical screening strategies (JNCI 2026)
  • Clinical and lifetime natural history of oral cancer (JNCI 2026)
  • Spatial dependence in latent susceptibility and joint damage in psoriatic arthritis (Biometrics 2023)
Evidence for Cancer Prevention

Working with clinical and epidemiological collaborators, I turn observational data into evidence for prevention and management guidelines:

  • Immediate versus delayed treatment of CIN2, estimated by target trial emulation (Ann Intern Med 2026)
  • Markers of CIN2 regression and subsequent risk of CIN3+ (JNCI 2026)
  • Quitting betel quid, smoking, and alcohol and the risk of oral precancer and cancer (JCO Glob Oncol 2026)

Collaboration & Consulting

Impactful public health research is a team effort.
I enjoy partnering with investigators across the health sciences at every stage of a study, from design and grant proposals to analysis and publication. I can be especially helpful when measurements are expensive, follow-up is incomplete, or disease unfolds over several stages.
Challenges I Can Help With
Costly measurements
When a test, scan, or assay is too expensive to run on everyone, I design studies that choose who to measure so the budget goes further.
From my work: evaluating new cervical cancer screening tests using archived samples (Biometrics 2026)
Follow-up at clinic visits
When people are seen only at scheduled visits, the exact time something happened is unknown. I use methods that account for this rather than guessing.
From my work: modeling the onset of joint damage in psoriatic arthritis (Biometrics 2023)
Disease that moves through stages
When a condition can progress, persist, or improve over time, multistate models describe how people move between stages and what affects those moves.
From my work: tracing how HPV infection progresses to, or clears from, cervical precancer (Biostatistics 2026)
Comparing strategies using observational studies
When a randomized trial isn't possible, I design analyses of observational data, such as cohorts, registries, and health records, that mimic a trial as closely as possible.
From my work: immediate versus delayed treatment of cervical precancer (Annals of Internal Medicine 2026)
Evaluating tests and markers
I assess how well a test or marker identifies who is at higher risk and who can safely be monitored.
From my work: markers of cervical precancer regression and later risk (JNCI 2026)
Risk factors over time
I estimate how exposures and behaviors shape disease risk over the long term, including what happens after people change them.
From my work: quitting betel quid, smoking, and alcohol and the risk of oral cancer (JCO Global Oncology 2026)
Does Your Data Look Like This?
  • People are only checked at scheduled visits, so you don't know exactly when something happened
  • Some participants drop out, or haven't had the outcome yet when the study ends
  • A key test, scan, or assay is too costly to run on everyone
  • The condition moves through stages, and can get better as well as worse
  • You want to compare treatments, but only have records instead of a trial
If any of these sound familiar, these are exactly the problems my methods are built for.

Study Design & Planning
  • Cost-efficient biomarker sub-studies within existing cohorts and biobanks: two-phase, case-cohort, and nested case-control sampling
  • Statistical analysis plans and design sections for grant proposals
  • Sample size and power calculations, including for time-to-event and multistate endpoints
Data Analysis
  • Survival (time-to-event) analysis under various censoring and truncation mechanisms: right-, left-, and interval-censored, current status, and left- or right-truncated data
  • Multistate modeling of disease progression, regression, and transitions between clinical states
  • Evaluation of screening and diagnostic tests: accuracy, risk stratification, and management thresholds
  • Target trial emulation for comparing treatment or surveillance strategies with observational data
  • Risk prediction modeling and validation using registry, EHR, and cohort data
